# Notation and Language Conventions

Two sources define what notation is worth. The CED defines it as content, through the mathematical practice skills of practice 4, communication and notation, and through the notation named inside essential knowledge statements. The scoring guidelines define it as points, through notation points, units points, interpretation points, and through the eligibility rules that decide when loose notation costs something. Citations name the cached document and page, so `sg-25:7` is the cached 2025 scoring guidelines at page 7 and `ced:72` is the cached CED at page 72. The scoring guidelines open every question with the note that the model solution is presented using standard mathematical notation (sg-25:2, sg-26:2).

## Practice 4 as the CED requirement [verified]

Practice 4 is communication and notation. Its five skills are the CED's own statement of what notation is assessed, and four of the five are marked assessed on the free response section.

| Id | CED code | Text | Assessed |
|---|---|---|---|
| BC-MPS-4A | 4.A | Use precise mathematical language. | yes |
| BC-MPS-4B | 4.B | Use appropriate units of measure. | yes |
| BC-MPS-4C | 4.C | Use appropriate mathematical symbols and notation (e.g., Represent a derivative using f'(x), y', and dy/dx). | yes |
| BC-MPS-4D | 4.D | Use appropriate graphing techniques. | yes |
| BC-MPS-4E | 4.E | Apply appropriate rounding procedures. | yes |

BC-MPS-4C carries the only notation example the CED prints inside a practice skill, naming three derivative notations as equivalent representations of the same object (ced:17). BC-MPS-4B is the units skill and is the practice code the rubrics attach to units points. BC-MPS-4E is the rounding skill and is the practice code behind the three-decimal rule. BC-MPS-4A, precise mathematical language, is the code behind interpretation and labelling points. Practice 3 supplies the two codes that pair with these on rubrics: BC-MPS-3F, explaining the meaning of mathematical solutions in context, and BC-MPS-3E, providing reasons or rationales. A single scoring point may carry more than one code, for example a point tagged 3.F and 4.B for an interpretation with units and a point tagged 1.D and 4.C for a definite integral (ced:218).

## Derivative notation [verified]

BC-EK-CHA-2B3 names three notations for the derivative of y equal to f of x: dy by dx, f prime of x, and y prime. BC-EK-FUN-3F2 extends this to higher orders, giving d squared y over d x squared, f double prime of x, and y double prime for the second derivative, and d to the n y over d x to the n or f superscript n of x for the nth (ced:80). The chain rule is presented in Leibniz form, dy/dx equal to dy/du multiplied by du/dx, because that form records the two dependencies (ced:72). Inside a related rates problem every variable is differentiated with respect to the same independent variable, which is why the chain rule is the stated basis of the topic (BC-EK-CHA-3D1).

The rubrics accept the three forms interchangeably and accept loose forms beyond them. sg-25:7 lists two forms as correct communication, then states that responses with incorrect communication, including a general derivative function equated to a value, a bare primed symbol equated to a value, and a derivative written without an evaluation bar, are nonetheless sufficient to earn the point. sg-24:8 accepts a Leibniz form in place of prime notation. The one shape that is charged is the general expression equated to a single number, which the equal sign section below treats.

In contexts the letters are conventional rather than required. Rectilinear motion uses x of t or s of t for position, v of t for velocity, a of t for acceleration, and absolute value bars for speed. Inverse function derivatives are written with g as the inverse of f and the value f prime of g of a in the denominator of a single fraction.

## Integral notation and the differential [verified]

The definite integral is written with the integral sign carrying a lower and an upper limit, an integrand, and a differential naming the variable. The Riemann sum that defines it is written in sigma notation from i equal to 1 to n with delta x sub i for the width of the ith subinterval and a sample point in each subinterval, and BC-EK-LIM-5A2 allows uniform and nonuniform partitions with L sub n, R sub n, M sub n, and T sub n as the four named sums. Evaluation is written F of b minus F of a, often with a vertical evaluation bar. The bound variable inside an accumulation function is a dummy variable and is distinct from the upper limit, which is what BC-PRQ-06013 records.

The differential is normally optional in scoring. The recurring phrase is "with or without the differential", applied at sg-25:7 to both polar area integrand points, at sg-25:14 to an integrand point, at sg-26:4 to an integral point, at sg-26:9 to an average value integral, and at sg-26:19 to an arc length integral and a volume integral. sg-22:2 goes further and states that a response missing the differential or using the wrong variable letter is eligible to earn the point. The exception is where the differential carries information the rubric needs later: sg-23:8 and sg-22:8 each list four cases for a missing differential in an accumulation expression, and the cases differ in whether the answer point stays available once the missing differential is combined with a value or an initial condition written on the wrong side.

Limits of integration are policed more tightly than the differential. sg-22:18 states a definite integral with incorrect bounds does not earn either point of its part, sg-22:8 states an indefinite integral is likewise not eligible for the answer point, and sg-23:16 and sg-23:17 both state that incorrect limits after a substitution block the answer point. Inside integration by parts the rubrics relax this: limits may be present, omitted, or partially present (sg-23:18, sg-22:18), and a tabular arrangement with unlabelled columns is accepted.

Absolute value inside a logarithmic antiderivative is optional. sg-26:13 accepts either parentheses or absolute value, sg-23:12 states an antiderivative written without absolute value symbols is eligible for all four points, and sg-21:20 accepts either form.

## Limit notation [verified]

BC-EK-LIM-1A1 fixes the written form: the limit of f of x as x approaches c equals R, with one sided forms marking the approach from the left and from the right. BC-EK-LIM-1B1 states that a limit expressed graphically, numerically, or analytically makes the same claim. Infinite limits and limits at infinity are written with the variable increasing or decreasing without bound and are read as vertical and horizontal asymptote statements.

Limit notation is the one place the guidelines score notation as a point in its own right, and the point lives on improper integrals and end behaviour. sg-23:17 states that to earn the point a response must correctly use limit notation throughout the problem and not include arithmetic with infinity, and gives two rejected forms. sg-22:19 places the same requirement on the answer point. sg-26:20 accepts limit notation applied either to the integral or to an antiderivative of the correct form, lists three acceptable shapes, banks the point once earned so that later arithmetic with infinity does not remove it, and states that a response presenting only an expression with arithmetic with infinity does not earn it, because the point is for correctly using limit notation. sg-25:4 states that for the end-behaviour value point, arithmetic with infinity is treated as scratch work and is not considered in scoring, and that a response taking a limit of the function rather than its derivative is not eligible for the value point. Limit notation is also required in the setup of a limit comparison (sg-21:23), and the same discipline runs from improper integrals into the integral test (sg-21:22). The scoring point ids are BC-PT-99053 for the improper integral and BC-PT-99054 for end behaviour.

## Units language [verified]

The unit of a derivative is the unit of the function divided by the unit of the independent variable (BC-EK-CHA-3A3), so a second derivative carries the unit of the function divided by the square of the unit of the variable, as in words per minute per minute (sg-25:11). The unit of the area between a rate graph and the axis is the unit of the rate multiplied by the unit of the independent variable (BC-EK-CHA-4A4), which is why an accumulated quantity carries the units of the quantity itself. An average value carries the units of the integrand, while an average rate of change carries the units of the function divided by the units of the input.

Units are scored where the prompt asks for them and are not read where it does not. BC-PT-99006 is the units point: correct units earn it whether or not they are attached to a numerical value (sg-25:11, sg-26:2), equivalent compact forms such as birds per day squared are accepted (sg-26:2), and units with no presented value at all do not earn it where the rubric ties them to a value (sg-22:13). sg-21:11, sg-22:15, sg-22:16 and sg-26:4 each state units are not required and are not considered in those parts. The command verbs that trigger the demand are BC-CV-11 and BC-CV-12.

## Interpretation sentence patterns [verified]

Two interpretation point types carry the sentence shape.

BC-PT-99007, interpretation of an accumulated quantity, is earned by a sentence naming the accumulated quantity in context together with the time or spatial interval (sg-26:3, sg-23:2). It is not earned by an interpretation that omits the interval, or that restates the integral without naming the quantity (sg-26:3). The pattern is therefore: the named quantity, what happened to it, by how much, over the stated interval. sg-26:3 also states the response need not include units of time or the location name, so the unit demand on this point varies by year, and the point can be earned whether or not the approximation points were earned.

BC-PT-99008, interpretation of a derivative value, is earned by a sentence giving the rate of change of the named quantity, its value, its units, and the instant it applies to (sg-21:3, sg-23:4). It is not earned when the sign language contradicts the presented value, such as describing a quantity as decreasing at a rate of a negative number (sg-23:4). A presented numerical value must exist before the point is available.

The suggested practice skill for a contextual interpretation topic is 3.F, explaining the meaning of mathematical solutions in context (ced:91), normally paired with 4.B for the units. The command verb is BC-CV-10.

## Justification language [verified]

Justify, give a reason, and give reasons are three different demands. A part saying to justify carries a justification point that requires a global argument (sg-25:5, sg-25:19, sg-23:15). A part saying to give a reason carries a reason point that one correctly targeted sentence satisfies (sg-25:17, sg-26:15, sg-24:4). The plural form carries one point per conclusion (sg-26:17). Naming the theorem is optional but must be correct when done (sg-25:12, sg-26:5, sg-22:14), and a derivative test need not be named (sg-26:8). The 2025 guideline refused a second derivative test the justification point where a global argument was demanded (sg-25:19), while the 2024 guideline accepted a second derivative evaluation for classifying a critical point of a solution of a differential equation (sg-24:10). The command verbs are BC-CV-06, BC-CV-07, BC-CV-08, BC-CV-09, and BC-CV-18.

## The equal sign and linkage [verified]

Two misuses of the equal sign are distinguished, and only one of them costs a point.

The first is equating a general expression to a single numerical value. sg-22:6 states a response with an incorrect equation of the form "function equals constant" will not earn the point, and adds that the response remains eligible for points for similar errors in later parts. sg-23:6 states a response that equates a general derivative expression to a numerical value earns only one of the two acceleration points for that reason.

The second is a broken equality chain between a correct integral and a correct answer, normally where a division or a scaling is written into the chain. sg-25:3 states that incorrect or unclear communication between the correct integral and the correct answer is treated as scratch work and is not considered in scoring, and gives three examples that each keep both points. sg-26:9 repeats the rule for a polar average value with three parallel examples, and sg-25:18 applies it to a label and a value. This reverses earlier practice: sg-23:4 and sg-22:3 each take one of two points for the same shape, and sg-23:16 withholds an answer point for an area sentence that equates an integral to a negated value.

Where an approximation is intended the equal sign is read charitably. sg-25:13 and sg-26:3 instruct readers to read an equals sign as an approximation sign for the sum form point, and sg-26:22 and sg-22:22 give the same instruction for series work.

Linkage failures are separated from arithmetic failures throughout. Banking protects a correct unsimplified expression from later errors (sg-25:13, sg-25:14, sg-26:3, sg-26:11, sg-25:25, sg-26:20). Simplification is optional, but attempted simplification must be correct (sg-26:14, sg-23:16, sg-22:13, sg-21:17). Parentheses errors are charged once and then explicitly not charged again in a later part (sg-23:6, sg-23:8).

## Labels [verified]

Labels are scored where the prompt asks for them, which is BC-CV-16. sg-25:18 states unlabelled values earn neither of two value points, then states that incorrect communication between a label and its answer is treated as scratch work, giving an example where a mislabelled integral still earns its point. sg-22:10 states unlabelled values are read left to right and top to bottom, and that a single value must be labelled to earn any points. Euler tables are labelled conditionally: sg-25:23 and sg-21:19 both state that with a correct answer present the table need not be labelled, and that with no answer or an incorrect answer the table must be correctly labelled. BC-PT-99064 is the labelled values point.

## Series notation [verified]

A series is written in sigma notation with an index, a starting value, a sub n for the general term, S sub n for the nth partial sum, and S for the value of a convergent series. A geometric series carries a and r and the closed form a over one minus r. Alternating series carry an alternating factor inside the general term, and absolute convergence work puts absolute value bars around it with the words absolutely and conditionally naming the classification. The ratio test is written with absolute value bars, limit notation, and the index substitution n to n plus one. Error bound work uses the word error, absolute value bars, the inequality symbol read as at most, and the index of the first omitted term. The interval of convergence is written in interval notation with open and closed endpoints, and the radius is a separate statement (BC-PRQ-10006, BC-PRQ-10008, BC-PRQ-10002).

Scoring treats series notation loosely in two places and tightly in two others. The ratio may be presented with or without absolute values, and a response that does not use the absolute value of a ratio can earn both the ratio and the limit points (sg-25:25). Extraneous terms are treated as scratch work and do not affect scoring (sg-26:22, sg-26:23), and terms may be presented in a list or as part of a polynomial or series (sg-25:26). Against that, a Taylor polynomial is scored on exactly the requested degree: any term of higher degree or a trailing ellipsis does not earn the remaining-term point (sg-25:22, sg-23:19, sg-23:21), and a correct expanded form rather than powers of the centre earns the first-terms point but not the remaining-term point (sg-25:22). The command verbs are BC-CV-21, BC-CV-25, BC-CV-26, and BC-CV-27.

## Vector notation [verified]

A planar position, velocity, or acceleration is written as an ordered pair of components, and ordered pair, angle bracket, and bracket notation are all acceptable provided the two components are identifiable. sg-23:6 states the acceleration vector may be presented with other bracket symbols and that the components may be listed separately as long as they are labelled. sg-22:7 states that if the vector is not presented as an ordered pair the two components must be labelled, and that reversed components lose both points. Each component carries its own constant of integration when a position is recovered from a velocity. Speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector, written as a square root of a sum of squared components, and each component derivative is parenthesised before being squared; sg-23:6 treats a missing parenthesis inside a squared component as an error that costs the setup point while leaving the answer point available, and notes that writing the sum of two squared derivative terms without an outer square root bracket is not treated as a parenthesis error. Calculator work on these questions must be in radian mode (sg-23:6). BC-PT-99050 is the speed point and BC-PT-99052 the acceleration vector point; BC-PRQ-09001 records the notation-reading prerequisite.

## Rounding and precision language [verified]

BC-MPS-4E is the rounding practice skill. Calculator-produced values are held to a three-decimal rule and rounding is a capped deduction rather than a block: the general note caps it at one point per question (sg-25:2, sg-26:2) and the individual notes implement the cap with the clause "unless an earlier point was not earned due to inappropriate rounding" (sg-25:4, sg-25:7, sg-25:8, sg-25:10, sg-25:23, sg-26:7, sg-26:8, sg-26:9). Candidate values inside a justification carry a looser rule, correct to the first digit after the decimal, rounded or truncated (sg-25:5, sg-25:9). A decimal presentation error can cost the answer point while leaving the justification point intact (sg-22:5). BC-CV-28 is the command verb that states a rounding target in the prompt.

## What notation never costs [verified]

The guidelines are explicit that several habits carry no penalty. Units are not required and are not read in parts that do not ask for them (sg-21:11, sg-22:15, sg-22:16, sg-26:4). The theorem behind a justification need not be named (sg-25:12, sg-26:5, sg-21:17). A derivative test need not be named (sg-26:8). Limits of integration may be present, omitted, or partially present inside integration by parts work (sg-23:18, sg-22:18). The tabular method is accepted for integration by parts with unlabelled columns (sg-23:18, sg-22:18). Extra identified critical values may be ignored (sg-26:8). Consideration of the interval endpoints does not affect scoring of a point of inflection answer (sg-25:17). Notation for the variable of integration is not policed (sg-22:5). Answers need not be simplified (sg-25:2, sg-26:2). The fuller treatment of each of these, with the rejected forms, is in ../scoring/notation-requirements.md.
